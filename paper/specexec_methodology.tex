\begin{abstract}
We present \textsc{SpecExec}, a pipeline whose goal is to \emph{discover small
speculative-execution leaks automatically}: a generator proposes instruction
sequences, a learned filter decides which candidates are worth checking, an
oracle decides whether they leak, and the oracle's verdicts retrain the
generator and the filter. Two learned components support the loop. The first
is a graph neural network (GINE) over program dependence graphs that classifies
assembly into nine classes (eight attack families and \cls{Benign}). The
second is a class- and architecture-conditioned Transformer that generates
candidate gadgets. Ground truth comes from three oracles: Spectector (symbolic
speculative non-interference, x86), InvisiSpec (gem5 execution), and
Revizor-based fuzzing on a bare-metal Intel i5-8300H.

The detector reaches $95.6 \pm 0.7\%$ accuracy in distribution (5 seeds), but
most of our contribution is in measuring what that number does and does not
mean. A label-conditioned screening defect in the test set, opcode shortcuts,
a length confound, and a CPU-specific instruction mix each inflated an earlier
result; we quantify each one and report what survives. A detector trained on
synthetic data does not recognise real hardware leaks. On 423 real Revizor
violations, a single lfence at the speculation boundary removes the leak in
every stable case for all four classes we tested (L1TF, MDS, Spectre~V1,
Spectre~V4), and fences far from the boundary do not. No model we trained
beats a non-learned rule on these mitigation labels yet.

On x86, oracle-in-the-loop rejection sampling raises the generator's
confirmed-leak rate from about 0.50 to 0.97 for Spectre~V1 and from 0.15 to
0.6--1.0 for Spectre~V4. It produces about 1,600 distinct leaking gadgets, and
self-supervised pretraining increases their diversity at a measured cost in
yield. The learned filter and the minimality stage are built but not yet
evaluated on real labels (\todo{ranker and minimality results}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Goal}
\label{sec:intro}

Speculative-execution attacks (Spectre, Meltdown/L1TF, MDS, Retbleed,
Inception, BHI) leak secrets through microarchitectural state that the
architectural semantics hide. Finding such leaks today relies on hand-written
gadget patterns per attack and per instruction set, on symbolic checkers that
model one speculation mechanism each, and on hardware fuzzers that are slow and
CPU-specific.

The goal of \textsc{SpecExec} is a learned pipeline that, given a description
of an instruction set, \emph{generates} instruction sequences likely to cause
speculative timing leaks; \emph{ranks} them with a regression model so that
only the most promising reach an expensive, precise oracle; and iterates
\emph{generate $\to$ filter $\to$ oracle $\to$ retrain} to discover the
\emph{smallest} leaking sequences. Table~\ref{tab:stages} maps each stage to
its current state.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item A declarative ISA specification that drives program-dependence-graph
    construction, checked against an independent assembler/disassembler
    oracle. That check, along with follow-up audits, found four bugs that had
    silently degraded every earlier result: x86 loads classified as stores,
    size-suffixed mnemonics falling into \textsc{other}, AT\&T operand
    direction, and ARM \texttt{blr}.
  \item A measured account of what the detector's accuracy means: test-set
    screening, opcode shortcuts, length confounding, cross-ISA transfer, and
    synthetic-to-real transfer, each quantified with multi-seed statistics.
  \item A hardware-labelling protocol on real Revizor violations. It
    establishes, on silicon, which lfence placements mitigate each class, and
    it supplies non-learned bars that any mitigation-aware model must beat.
  \item An oracle-verified generation loop on x86 that produces distinct
    leaking gadgets at high yield, with a powered (n=5) diversity/yield
    analysis of pretraining.
  \item Negative results reported as such: learned node features lose to
    hand features; the classifier fails as a generation filter; simulator
    oracles fail for store bypass.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Detection.}
Let $S = (i_1, \ldots, i_n)$ be a window of assembly instructions. The
detector is a classifier $f: S \mapsto \mathcal{C}$ with
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{C} = \{&\text{BENIGN}, \text{SPECTRE\_V1}, \text{V2}, \text{V4},\\
                  &\text{L1TF}, \text{MDS}, \text{RETBLEED},
                   \text{INCEPTION}, \text{BHI}\}.
\end{align*}
The task is assembly-only: no source, IR or runtime information.
Exploitability depends on the microarchitecture, so a static label is a
\emph{triage} signal for an oracle, not a verdict.

\paragraph{Discovery.}
A generator $g(\cdot \mid c, a)$ samples sequences for class $c$ and ISA $a$.
An oracle $O(S) \in \{\textsc{leak}, \textsc{safe}, \textsc{unrunnable}\}$
labels a candidate; an oracle call costs from seconds (Spectector) to tens of
minutes (InvisiSpec) or a hardware batch slot (Revizor). A filter $r(S)$
orders candidates so that confirmed leaks per oracle call are maximised. The
end product is a set of leaking sequences that are \emph{minimal}: removing any
instruction stops the leak.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Synthetic and compiled corpus (in-distribution)}
\label{sec:data-synth}

\paragraph{Internal handcrafted vulnerabilities.}
Hand-authored C files encoding canonical patterns per class
(\texttt{c\_vulns/}), compiled with GCC and Clang at O0--O3 for x86\_64 and
ARM64. Only about 21 of them are complete, runnable proofs of concept; the
remaining $\sim$1,400 \texttt{.s} files and 530 variant \texttt{.c} files are
pattern fragments without a secret-to-transmitter data flow. This is why the
oracles in Section~\ref{sec:oracles} matter: most training gadgets were never
executable leaks.

\paragraph{Compiler-synthesised samples (\texttt{p9\_*}).}
Known-vulnerable kernel and library code compiled with GCC and Clang for
x86\_64 and AArch64 at O0 and O1.

\paragraph{External sources.}
Google SafeSide~\cite{safeside} (200 samples, compiled for
\texttt{x86\_64}/\texttt{arm64}, O0--O3); the Spectector
benchmarks~\cite{spectector} (101 vanilla Kocher variants~\cite{kocher1998});
and up to 300 FastSpec~\cite{fastspec} synthetic V1 gadgets, used for
training only.

\paragraph{Benign code.}
Benign assembly from 1,031 GitHub repositories, compiled for ARM64. The
original corpus contained \emph{no x86 benign code}; Section~\ref{sec:xisa}
shows the consequence (x86 benign false-positive rate 98.4\%) and the fix: real
x86 and ARM benign functions compiled from mbedTLS, with a disjoint slice held
out for validation.

\paragraph{Splits.}
The locked test set (1,670 records; frozen since pipeline v53) and the v54/v55
training sets are summarised in Appendix~\ref{app:history}. A
length-matched, multi-scale training set
(\texttt{v54\_train\_lenmatch\_v4s}, 18,897 records before V4-family
additions) fixes the length confound of Section~\ref{sec:xisa}.

\subsection{Test-set screening defect and the unscreened split}
\label{sec:screening}

The v53 dataset builder applied a \emph{label-conditioned} filter
(\texttt{has\_train\_attack\_signal}) to the test pool, three lines after a
comment saying it did not. Every locked test record therefore satisfies a
hand-written rule keyed on its own label (e.g.\ an MDS record must contain
\texttt{verw}, \texttt{movntdqa} or \texttt{clflush}): 100\% conformance for
every class, against 41\% (\cls{Retbleed}) and 69\% (\cls{Inception}) on an
unscreened control. This is selective data snooping in the sense of Arp et
al.~\cite{arp2022}, and it guarantees a shortcut on the test set.

We fixed the builder: the label-conditioned filter now raises if it is called
on a non-training split. We also rebuilt an \emph{unscreened} test split
(1,017 records) from the pre-filter pool, using only label-independent quality
criteria. Table~\ref{tab:screening} gives the inflation. The flat feature
models lose 4--5\,pp; the GINE graph model loses 0.3\,pp, because the graph
structure survives the screening.

\subsection{Cross-ISA data}
\label{sec:data-riscv}

\paragraph{RISC-V corpus.}
498 files cross-compiled for riscv64. An ISA-independence test on
canonical-op bigrams (Section~\ref{sec:xisa}) shows this corpus is
\emph{transliterated from ARM64}, not written for RISC-V. In all 6 shared
classes it is closer to ARM than ARM is to x86 (sign test $p=0.016$). We
therefore treat it as a stand-in, not as idiomatic RISC-V. A held-out version
with corrected labels (\texttt{riscv\_loio\_corpus\_v2}, 248 records, 27
attacks) is used for evaluation.

\paragraph{Real RISC-V proofs of concept.}
11 records from 4 published RISC-V PoCs (BOOM RTL attacks; T-Head C910
silicon), stamped validation-only. This is the only RISC-V evidence
independent of our own corpus. In-order RISC-V cores (SiFive U74, T-Head
C906/C908) are empirically not vulnerable, so claims are scoped to
out-of-order cores.

\subsection{Real hardware violations (Revizor, i5-8300H)}
\label{sec:data-hw}

Revizor~\cite{revizor} fuzzing campaigns on a bare-metal Intel i5-8300H (Coffee
Lake) produced \textbf{423 unique violating programs}: Spectre~V1 130, L1TF 155,
MDS 83, Spectre~V4 55. Each class comes from its own Revizor configuration
(instruction categories and contract). A seed ledger prevents reusing a
program-generator seed, because a reused seed regenerates byte-identical
programs. Violating programs (Intel syntax, \texttt{r14}-relative sandbox) are
converted to the pipeline's AT\&T format with an assemble/disassemble round trip
(\texttt{clang} + \texttt{llvm-objdump}). The held-out split is by violation
(group), with disjoint generator seeds: V1 54, L1TF 64, MDS 33, V4 22. A
leakage audit (exact-sequence, group, seed and near-duplicate checks; maximum
held-out-to-train similarity 0.76) passes.

\section{Oracles}
\label{sec:oracles}

Every claim that a sequence leaks comes from one of the oracles below, never
from our own classifier.

\subsection{Spectector (symbolic, x86)}
Spectector~\cite{spectector} checks speculative non-interference over x86
assembly. It is deterministic and runs in a container (Docker for development,
Apptainer on the cluster). A per-gadget timeout of at least 300\,s is needed,
because Spectre~V1 data checks take 25--40\,s and a 30\,s limit times out real
leaks. Spectector models conditional-branch (PHT) speculation. In our
multi-class runs it can rule on 82\% of generated V1 gadgets and 87\% of V4
gadgets, but only 15\% of V2 and 0.2\% of Retbleed gadgets. It cannot model
L1TF/MDS faults. The continuous \texttt{trace\_length} it reports measures the
size of the symbolic formula, not the severity of the leak.

\subsection{gem5 and InvisiSpec (execution)}
Stock gem5 v24 (O3 CPU, classic caches) does \emph{not} leave a
misspeculated-load cache footprint: squashed loads are discarded at
translation, so Flush+Reload never sees a signal. We rejected it as an
oracle. The 2019 InvisiSpec~\cite{invisispec} gem5 fork with classic caches
does leak Spectre~V1: it recovers all 40 secret bytes of a tuned PoC. It does
\emph{not} reproduce store bypass. An earlier ``V4 leaks 40/40'' result was a
measurement bug: the harness counted confident cache hits rather than checking
that the recovered byte matched the secret. With byte matching, it recovers
0/40 correct bytes. Lesson: a Flush+Reload verdict must compare the recovered
value with the secret.

\subsection{Revizor on real hardware (i5-8300H)}
Revizor~\cite{revizor} runs randomly generated programs on the real CPU through
a kernel module and reports a \emph{violation} when hardware traces differ for
inputs that a speculation contract says are equivalent. Controls used:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{MDS and L1TF} violations (3/184 and 3/53 test cases in the
    first campaign) persist with SMT disabled, ruling out sibling-thread
    noise.
  \item \textbf{Spectre~V4}: a revised configuration (straight-line code,
    store$\to$load pairs) found 15 violations across 5 seeds. Enabling the
    speculative-store-bypass mitigation (SSBP MSR) gives 0; disabling SMT
    gives 16. Every violating program has a single basic block and no
    conditional branch, so it is store bypass, not V1.
\end{itemize}
Revizor runs in batches and only on the bare-metal machine, so it is the most
expensive oracle per candidate.

\subsection{Hardware labels for fenced variants}
\label{sec:hwlabel}

Training a model to recognise \emph{mitigated} code requires knowing that a
fence mitigates. Our first ``mitigated twins'' were structural assumptions
(Section~\ref{sec:hw-mitig}), so we replaced them with hardware labels.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
For each held-out violation we write fenced variants of the original
\texttt{program.asm}, keeping labels intact
(\texttt{oracle/revizor/scripts/hw\_label\_variants.py}):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \texttt{original} (positive control);
  \item \texttt{fence\_all} (an lfence after every instruction);
  \item \texttt{twin} (the class boundary: V1 at the start of the jcc's taken
    block; V4 after each store; MDS/L1TF before each load);
  \item \texttt{shifted} (V1 before the jcc; V4 before each store);
  \item \texttt{after\_load} (MDS/L1TF);
  \item \texttt{v1\_fallthrough} (the jcc's exit path);
  \item \texttt{entry} and \texttt{tail}, with the same number of fences as
    the twin.
\end{itemize}
Each variant is re-run with \texttt{rvzr reproduce} three times on the
violation's own inputs and configuration, with Revizor's speculation and
observation filters turned \emph{off}: a filtered test case would read as ``no
violation'' and be mistaken for ``mitigated''. A variant is \emph{vulnerable}
if it violates in 3/3 runs and \emph{mitigated} if in 0/3; anything else is
flaky. A violation directory whose unmodified original does not reproduce 3/3
is unstable and contributes no labels. Revizor's parser forbids an instruction
directly after a jump, so such placements are skipped. We checked that every
program-level variant converts to exactly the sequence-level training variant,
apart from jump offsets, which shift by the bytes the fences add.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:hwlabel} (3,024 reproduce calls; 806 labelled records).
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{A fence at the class boundary mitigates every class}, in every
    stable directory. For V4 this is the first evidence that an lfence, and
    not only the SSBD MSR, removes these leaks.
  \item \textbf{Fences far from the leak do not mitigate}: entry/tail
    variants stay vulnerable in 289/316 stable cases.
  \item \textbf{The jcc's exit path does not mitigate V1}: 32/32 non-flaky
    directories stay vulnerable.
  \item \textbf{A fence just \emph{before} the jcc or store also mitigates}
    (41/41 and 21/21). A plausible mechanism, not yet tested: in Revizor's
    programs the branch condition and the store address come from loads, and
    a fence waits for those loads, so the branch resolves (or the store
    address is known) before anything can speculate past it.
\end{itemize}
Stability: 9 of 54 V1 originals do not reproduce 3/3, and V1 misplaced
variants are flaky in 7--10 of 41 directories. MDS, L1TF and V4 are clean.

\paragraph{Mid-sequence counterexamples (in progress).}
In this variant set every non-mitigating fence sits at the start or end of the
sequence, and every mitigating one sits inside it. A non-learned rule,
``\cls{Benign} iff some lfence is inside the sequence'', therefore matches
799/806 labels (0.991). The set cannot tell structural understanding from
fence position. We added four counterfactual variants that put a fence
\emph{inside} the sequence where it may not mitigate:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \texttt{cf\_wrong\_path} (V1): \texttt{jcc; .bb\_new: lfence; jmp}.
    In AT\&T form this is exactly our pre-October V1 twin.
  \item \texttt{cf\_nonleak}: a fence next to memory operations that
    \texttt{rvzr minimize} found unnecessary for the violation.
  \item \texttt{cf\_after\_bypass} (V4): a fence after the load that follows
    the essential store.
  \item \texttt{cf\_fencepass}: Revizor's fence-insertion pass, which keeps
    every fence the violation survives.
\end{itemize}

\section{ISA Specification and Graph Construction}
\label{sec:spec}

\subsection{Declarative ISA specification}
Classification logic (opcode categories, memory-access types, speculation
flags, def/use registers, edge windows, realisation parameters) lives in
per-ISA JSON specifications (\texttt{spec/x86\_64.json},
\texttt{arm64.json}, \texttt{riscv.json}) over a shared \texttt{base}
document. A generic engine with no ISA knowledge of its own interprets them.
The spec was first exported from the hand-built PDG builder and verified
identical on the full corpus (25,942 instruction pairs; 207,113 nodes and
437,231 edges; zero mismatches). Because an exported spec matches its source by
construction, that check proves refactoring fidelity, not correctness.

\subsection{Independent oracle gate and the bugs it and its follow-ups found}
\label{sec:spec-bugs}
For correctness, each instruction is assembled with \texttt{llvm-mc}, and its
control-flow class is read back with \texttt{capstone}. Agreement was 98.80\%
at first (274 defects), and is 99.86\% on the current specs. A repeatable gate
(\texttt{scripts/run\_feature\_gate.sh}) must pass before any spec or feature
change is trusted. The gate and the audits that followed it found:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{ARM \texttt{blr}} indirect calls fell into \textsc{other}.
    Fixing it raised Spectre~V2 recall from 76\% to 94\% (+1.32\,pp accuracy).
  \item \textbf{x86 loads classified as stores.} In AT\&T syntax one mnemonic
    (\texttt{movq}) serves both directions, and the store rule was checked
    first. 58.5\% of unambiguous x86 memory instructions were misclassified,
    which made the secret-source and transmitter flags effectively dead on x86
    (0.01\% vs.\ 1.00\% on ARM). Fixed by an operand-order field in the spec
    (0.87\% after the fix). The control-flow oracle gate \emph{cannot see this
    class of bug}, because it covers only CALL/RET/JUMP.
  \item \textbf{Size-suffixed mnemonics} (\texttt{addq}, \texttt{pushq},
    \ldots) missed word-boundary patterns: 28\% of all instructions landed in
    \textsc{other}. Fixed to below 0.3\% on all three ISAs.
  \item \textbf{AT\&T destination direction} (the first register taken as the
    destination), sub-register aliasing (\texttt{eax}/\texttt{rax},
    \texttt{w0}/\texttt{x0}, \texttt{a0}/\texttt{x10}), and data dependence
    from the last three definitions instead of the reaching definition.
    A probe classifier that predicts the ISA from each input channel dropped
    from 0.98 to 0.82 (categories) and from 0.86 to 0.75 (graph) after these
    fixes. The remaining ISA signal is real (e.g.\ RISC-V's fused
    compare-and-branch).
\end{itemize}
Every result produced before these fixes is re-measured or withdrawn. The
in-distribution accuracy in Section~\ref{sec:indist} is on current code.

\subsection{Program dependence graph}
\label{sec:pdg}
Each window becomes a PDG with one node per instruction. Node features
(41 dims): opcode-category one-hot (19), memory-access type (5), register
degree (2), and 14 binary speculation flags (\texttt{is\_branch},
\texttt{is\_secret\_source}, \texttt{is\_transmitter}, \texttt{is\_lfence},
\ldots). There are nine edge types:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item DATA\_DEP: reaching definitions;
  \item CONTROL\_FLOW;
  \item SPEC\_CONDITIONAL, SPEC\_INDIRECT and SPEC\_RETURN: from a
    speculation source to the instructions in its window;
  \item MEMORY\_ORDER;
  \item CACHE\_TEMPORAL;
  \item FENCE\_BOUNDARY;
  \item RSB\_CHAIN: call/return pairs.
\end{itemize}
Store bypass has no reliable static signature: an SSB edge we built also fired
on $-$O0 Spectre~V1, which spills its index through the stack, so we
reverted it. \todo{decide and document whether address-based MEMORY\_ORDER
edges (always on since commit \texttt{e3a541e}) are intended; rerun the gate.}

\subsection{Tokenisation and learned instruction features}
Instructions are normalised: registers become \texttt{<reg>}, immediates
\texttt{<imm>}, memory operands \texttt{<mem>}/\texttt{<mem-idx>}, and
neutralised call targets \texttt{<fn>}. Three vocabularies exist:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \emph{mnemonic}: literal mnemonics; 92.7\% out-of-vocabulary on RISC-V;
  \item \emph{canonical}: 53 ISA-neutral operation names declared in the spec
    (\texttt{addq}/\texttt{adds}/\texttt{addi} $\to$ \texttt{ADD}); 7.9--9.1\%
    out-of-vocabulary on RISC-V;
  \item \emph{neutral}: 0.0\% out-of-vocabulary on RISC-V.
\end{itemize}
A masked-language-model Transformer~\cite{devlin2019} trained on x86 and ARM
yields per-instruction embeddings (\texttt{spec/mlm\_neutral.pt}). Token
\emph{frequencies} still identify the ISA (0.98), so the locked model adds an
adversarial architecture head (Section~\ref{sec:detector}).

\subsection{Inline features}
The 58 handcrafted inline features (opcode fractions, structural ratios,
pattern densities, primitive flags, page-probe detection;
Appendix~\ref{app:inline}) include many ISA literals. On RISC-V, 43 of the 58 are
identically zero. A \emph{neutral} subset of 9 features that fire on every ISA
is used in the cross-ISA configuration.

\section{Detector}
\label{sec:detector}

\subsection{Architecture}
The backbone is GINE~\cite{hu2020strategies}:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  h_v^{(k)} &= \text{MLP}^{(k)}\Bigl((1+\varepsilon^{(k)}) h_v^{(k-1)} \\
   &\quad + \!\!\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}\!\! \text{ReLU}\bigl(h_u^{(k-1)} + s_{t} e_{uv}\bigr)\Bigr)
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
with learnable per-edge-type scales $s_t$. It uses three layers, hidden size
128, batch normalisation, residual connections, a gated virtual node,
Jumping-Knowledge concatenation~\cite{xu2018jk} and attention readout. The
graph embedding is fused with an MLP over the inline features, global features
(5) and an architecture embedding (8), then classified by a two-layer MLP. The
loss is class-weighted cross-entropy plus supervised
contrastive~\cite{khosla2020supervised} (13 hard-negative pairs, warm-up) plus
an auxiliary inline-feature head. The optimiser is AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}
($10^{-3}$), with early stopping on a group-stratified validation split (the
group is the source file).

\paragraph{Configurations.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{B1} (in-distribution reference): hand node features,
    spec builder, architecture embedding, 58 inline features.
  \item \textbf{No-hand baseline}: architecture embedding, no inline features,
    trained on the trigger-masked set (Section~\ref{sec:shortcut}).
  \item \textbf{Locked cross-ISA model}
    (\texttt{models/locked\_classifier.json}, locked 2026-09-25): learned
    neutral-MLM node features, the 9 neutral inline features, NOPs dropped,
    node dropout 0.1, an adversarial architecture head ($\lambda=1$), and
    length-matched training data. It is a 5-seed ensemble (mean softmax),
    loaded with SHA-256 verification.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Augmentation}
Nine augmentation transforms preserve the attack invariant, and a 143-test
suite verifies them: register rename, NOP insertion, local swap, immediate
perturbation, stride-synonym swap, idiom substitution, branch-polarity flip,
barrier upgrade (never downgrade) and slice recomposition. The suite found six
implementation bugs (Appendix~\ref{app:history}). Separately,
\emph{trigger-masked} augmentation replaces class-defining opcodes so that the
model cannot rely on them (Section~\ref{sec:shortcut}).

\section{Detector Results}
\label{sec:detresults}

\subsection{In-distribution}
\label{sec:indist}
On the locked test set, retrained on current code (after all
Section~\ref{sec:spec-bugs} fixes), B1 reaches \textbf{95.62\,$\pm$\,0.67\%}
accuracy over 5 seeds. An earlier multi-seed figure, 96.14\,$\pm$\,1.59\%
(macro-F1 85.6), predates the x86 load/store fix; the single best run
(97.07\%) is the top of that range, not a typical result. The locked
cross-ISA ensemble trades in-distribution accuracy for transfer: 92.45\%.
Two caveats bound these numbers:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{Screening} inflates the flat models by 4--5\,pp and GINE by
    0.3\,pp (Table~\ref{tab:screening}).
  \item \textbf{Leakage-controlled splits.} Under group hold-out (no base
    gadget on both sides), accuracy drops 4.9\,pp and macro-F1 7.9\,pp. Under
    an optimisation-level hold-out (train O0--O2/Os, test O3), macro-F1 falls
    from 94 to 39.
\end{itemize}
Cross-optimisation generalisation for attack classes is weak, and the random
split overstates it. \todo{re-measure group and optimisation hold-outs on
current code with the locked model.}

\subsection{Opcode shortcuts}
\label{sec:shortcut}
\paragraph{The shortcut is load-bearing.}
Replacing each class's trigger opcodes with \texttt{nop} (same length, 442 of
1,670 records altered) drops the flat hand-58 model from 95.15\% to 78.44\%.
MDS falls from 97.8\% to 0\%, V4 from 99.2\% to 0\%, and L1TF from 67.6\% to
0\%. \cls{Benign} and Spectre~V1, whose rules are structural, do not move.
For these classes the flat model \emph{is} the hand rule.

\paragraph{Removing it.}
Training with trigger-masked augmentation closes the gap between intact and
masked inputs from 27.4\,pp to 1.1\,pp. Masked macro-F1 rises from 0.503 to
0.805, and masked calibration error falls from 0.102 to 0.027.

\paragraph{Hand features as an x86 crutch.}
With the 58 inline features removed (5 seeds), macro-F1 improves on every
axis:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item locked: 0.813 $\to$ 0.869;
  \item ARM64 subset: 0.618 $\to$ 0.752;
  \item x86 subset: 0.897 $\to$ 0.910;
  \item masked: 0.803 $\to$ 0.859.
\end{itemize}
A domain-adversarial (DANN) architecture head \emph{hurt} in that setting
(ARM64 0.752 $\to$ 0.456) and was retired there. The locked model's
adversarial head is a different configuration (neutral learned features), and
it was selected on held-out RISC-V.

\subsection{Learned vs.\ hand features (negative result)}
\label{sec:learned}
A ten-seed paired comparison on current code (learned node features vs.\
hand, full GINE) finds the learned features significantly \emph{worse}:
$-1.66$\,pp accuracy ($p=0.001$), $-3.47$\,pp macro-F1 ($p=0.008$), and
Spectre~V2 recall $-9.55$\,pp ($p=0.029$). An earlier per-class L1TF lift of
+11.9\,pp did not replicate; with 10 seeds the lift is 0.00\,pp. A
pre-registered TOST equivalence test~\cite{schuirmann1987} also rejects
equivalence ($-2.58$\,pp, 90\% CI $[-3.81,-1.35]$). Spec-generic flat features
beat hand-58 on the locked split (+1.88\,pp) but lose under group hold-out
($-1.55$\,pp, CI $[-2.01,-1.08]$); the locked-split win is an artifact. The
value of the specification is portability (no ISA literals in code), not
accuracy.

\subsection{Cross-ISA transfer}
\label{sec:xisa}
\paragraph{The length confound.}
The training \cls{Benign} class consisted of 25--30-instruction ARM windows,
while RISC-V benign test code is whole functions, so the model learned ``long
$\Rightarrow$ attack''. A model retrained on current code and scored on
held-out RISC-V had these false-positive rates by length:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item $\le$30 instructions: 12\%;
  \item 31--60: 1\%;
  \item 61--120: 20\%;
  \item $>$120: 89\%.
\end{itemize}
The same gap hit x86: with no x86 benign training data, 98.4\% of real x86
benign functions were flagged as attacks (ARM: 27.4\%). Length-matched
training data and real x86/ARM benign code fix the cause. Windowed inference
alone lowers RISC-V benign false positives from 28.6\% to 3.0\%, but also
lowers detection from 74.8\% to 54.8\%.

\paragraph{Locked model on held-out RISC-V.}
On \texttt{riscv\_loio\_corpus\_v2} ($n=248$, 27 attacks; cluster-bootstrap
95\% CIs):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item benign false-positive rate: 2.3\% [0.4, 4.8];
  \item attack detection: 48.1\% [17.9, 77.1];
  \item Youden's $J$: 0.46.
\end{itemize}
Per class:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item BHI 0.75;
  \item RSB 0.33;
  \item V1 0.25;
  \item V4 0.00 (store bypass does not transfer across ISAs);
  \item Retbleed, Inception, L1TF and MDS: unscored (no valid RISC-V
    examples).
\end{itemize}
Canonical learned features (no hand features) fail to transfer ($J=-0.06$),
probably because RISC-V's three-operand compare-and-branch is out of
vocabulary, which hides the V1 branch itself.

\paragraph{With RISC-V training data.}
Mixing the RISC-V corpus into training (group-stratified) raises held-out
RISC-V accuracy from 24.85\,$\pm$\,7.36\% to 75.45\,$\pm$\,13.20\% (5 seeds),
with no x86/ARM regression (95.89\,$\pm$\,1.69\% vs.\ 94.83\,$\pm$\,1.50\%).
Spectre~V1 and V4 remain unmeasurable on that split for lack of data. The
corpus is transliterated (Section~\ref{sec:data-riscv}), so this measures
adaptation to RISC-V surface form, not idiomatic RISC-V attacks.

\paragraph{Real RISC-V PoCs.}
An earlier spec-builder checkpoint scored 0/11 on the real PoC set
(Spectre~V1 0/7, previously unmeasurable). \todo{score the locked ensemble on
the 11 real RISC-V PoC records.}

\subsection{Synthetic-to-real transfer (hardware gadgets)}
\label{sec:hw-transfer}
Results are over 5 seeds, on the 173 held-out real violations
(Section~\ref{sec:data-hw}).

\paragraph{Synthetic training does not transfer.}
A detector trained only on synthetic and compiled data labels most real
gadgets Spectre~V1, whatever their class. For example, it calls 74\% of real
MDS, 56\% of L1TF and 73\% of real V4 gadgets V1.

\paragraph{Per-class real-data models learn style.}
A model given real Revizor programs of only its own class labels real programs
of \emph{every} class as its own: ``Revizor-generated $\Rightarrow$ my class''.
These models' 1.00 recall is withdrawn.

\paragraph{Joint real-data model vs.\ an opcode bar.}
One model trained on all four classes' real gadgets separates them on held-out
data at 0.97--1.00. A standard-scaled bag-of-opcodes logistic regression gets
0.97 (L1TF 0.98, V1 0.93): each Revizor configuration's instruction set gives
the class away. The graph model adds about +0.01 (L1TF) and +0.05 (V1). An
earlier ``+0.25--0.30 over opcodes'' came from an unscaled baseline and is
withdrawn. Class identity and Revizor configuration stay confounded until the
classes are collected under a shared instruction set (\todo{shared-config
Revizor campaign}).

\subsection{Mitigation awareness against hardware labels}
\label{sec:hw-mitig}
Two models were trained with \emph{structural} fenced twins:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{allhw}: twins labelled \cls{Benign}.
  \item \textbf{allhw2}: twins labelled \cls{Benign}, plus misplaced-fence
    variants labelled as attacks. Some of those labels later proved wrong
    (Section~\ref{sec:hwlabel}): the old V1 twin fenced the exit path, and
    the shifted placements mitigate.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:hwscore} scores both models against the hardware labels.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item allhw learned ``has an lfence $\Rightarrow$ safe''.
  \item allhw2 reproduces its training labels exactly, including the wrong
    ones. It fails only on the fixed V1 twin (0.00) and the shifted variants
    (V1 0.00, V4 0.01); the bottleneck was label correctness, not model
    capacity.
  \item No model beats either non-learned rule. The interior-fence rule

\end{itemize}

mid-sequence counterexamples. A structure claim requires it to beat the

\section{Generator}
\label{sec:gen}

\subsection{Model and realisation}
A decoder-only Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017} ($d=128$, 3 layers, 4 heads) is
trained by next-token prediction on normalised instruction tokens. Each
sequence is prefixed with a class token and an architecture token, so sampling
from $[\langle\textsc{cls}\rangle_c, \langle\textsc{arch}\rangle_a]$ conditions on
both. Base training uses 5,532 real gadget records. Optional self-supervised
pretraining uses 200k compiled C function bodies (AnghaBench~\cite{anghabench})
for x86\_64, ARM64 and riscv64. A spec-driven \emph{realiser} instantiates
operand placeholders with registers, immediates (biased toward attack
constants such as 64 and 4096) and addressing modes from the target ISA's spec.
For oracle checks, the realised body is \emph{spliced} into a fixed,
class-specific misdirection template (e.g.\ a bounds-check bypass) that ends in
a cache-line-scaled probe write.

\paragraph{Conditioning.}
A reference classifier fit on real data finds the requested class 7.4$\times$
more often than the class prior (class only), and 5.7$\times$ with class and
architecture. These numbers come from a classifier, not an oracle. An
ISA-purity figure of 96--98\% is likely optimistic: the purity metric was blind
to ARM load/store mnemonics (\texttt{ldr}, \texttt{str}, \ldots) appearing in
x86 output.

\subsection{Syntactic validity}
Initially only 2.3\% of generated sequences were fully valid (71.1\% of
instructions). The first oracle-splice run gave 3 leaks, 4 safe and 73
unrunnable out of 80. A triage by assembler diagnostic found realiser bugs,
fixed without retraining:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{An unimplemented \texttt{<fn>} placeholder}: per-instruction
    validity 71.1\% $\to$ 81.2\%.
  \item \textbf{Register-width mismatches} (64-bit registers paired with
    \texttt{l}/\texttt{w}/\texttt{b} suffixes): x86 per-instruction 82.9\%
    $\to$ 94.5\%, and per-sequence validity 3.5\% $\to$ 21.5\%.
  \item \textbf{Cross-ISA mnemonic leakage}: masking opcodes that the target
    ISA's spec rejects gives 24.2\% per sequence and 95.7\% per instruction
    on x86.
\end{itemize}
The remaining failures are \texttt{.L} label tokens used as data operands and
residual operand-type errors.

\subsection{Oracle-in-the-loop training}
Rejection-sampled fine-tuning repeats four steps: sample, realise and splice,
check with the oracle, then fine-tune on the confirmed leaks (learning rate
$10^{-4}$). Every realised sample, including unrunnable ones, is logged, so
yield has a denominator and diversity can be audited.

\paragraph{Spectre~V1 (x86, Spectector).}
The confirmed-leak yield rises from about 0.50 to about 0.97 in one round, then
plateaus, reproducibly across seeds. Auditing confirms genuine discovery
rather than mode collapse: 117--137 distinct leaking gadgets from about 172
leaks, with median pairwise similarity 0.32.

\paragraph{Pretraining: diversity at a yield cost (n=5).}
Two bugs initially made pretraining look useless:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{Tokenizer mismatch}: only 4 of 460 embeddings transferred
    (322/460 after the fix).
  \item \textbf{Fine-tune learning rate}: at $3\times10^{-3}$ the fine-tune
    overwrote the pretrained weights (fixed at $10^{-4}$).
\end{itemize}
With both fixed, Table~\ref{tab:pretrain} shows that pretraining strongly
reduces mode collapse and increases distinct leaking gadgets, at a significant
cost in raw yield. For discovery, where many distinct gadgets beat repeated
copies, this is the right trade.

\section{Filter / Ranker}
\label{sec:rank}

\subsection{The classifier is not a usable filter}
Two findings came from checking the classifier against the oracle data.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{V1 has no \textsc{safe} class.} Generated Spectre~V1 samples
    contain 1,531 leaks, 0 safe and 456 unrunnable. An earlier
    ``classifier vs.\ oracle AUC 0.44'' therefore measured leak vs.\
    \emph{unrunnable}, not leak vs.\ safe.
  \item \textbf{On V4 the classifier is inverted.} Spectre~V4 is the only
    class with both outcomes: 460 unique gadgets after de-duplication, 273
    leak and 187 safe. Results:
    \begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1em]
      \item the locked classifier's attack probability has AUC 0.14 (0.27
        within an RL round) and correlates $-0.66$ with sequence length;
      \item sequence length alone gives 0.75 (0.59 within round);
      \item an opcode-bag logistic regression, evaluated leaving one RL run
        out, gives 0.44.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
RL round confounds everything here: the V4 leak rate rises from 0.18 to 0.85
across rounds, so we report within-round AUC.

\subsection{Design}
The \texttt{rank/} package reuses the locked classifier as a frozen encoder,
with a forward hook on the fused representation. A regression head with
MC-dropout uncertainty sits on top, candidates are chosen by UCB, and the data
is split by token-content hash. After the findings above, the target becomes
$P(\text{runnable}) \cdot P(\text{leak} \mid \text{runnable})$. It sits behind
a cheap non-learned pre-check (assembles, uses only instructions the oracle
supports), and it is evaluated prospectively: train on rounds $\le r$, test
on round $r{+}1$ of held-out seeds. The metric is confirmed leaks per oracle
call, against random ordering and against the within-round length bar
(0.59). The head-room is largest early: a filter's best gain over random
ordering is about $1/\text{leak rate}$, about 5$\times$ for V4 round 0 and
about 1.14$\times$ for V1 by round 3.

\section{Minimality}
\label{sec:min}
The goal is the smallest leaking sequences. For real violations we run
Revizor's instruction-removal pass, which deletes instructions one at a time
from the end and keeps a deletion if the violation survives. This gives, for
each violation, the instructions it needs. For generated leaks, the plan is
delta debugging~\cite{ddmin} with the oracle as the test, with removals
ordered by a learned probability that the leak survives, in the spirit of
ProbDD~\cite{probdd}, to save oracle calls.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{One CPU, one fuzzer.} Hardware results come from one Coffee
    Lake part and Revizor. BHI likely needs a newer Intel part, and
    Inception affects only AMD Zen.
  \item \textbf{x86-only verification.} Spectector and our hardware are x86.
    ARM64/RISC-V generation is unverified.
  \item \textbf{Class--configuration confound} in the real corpus
    (Section~\ref{sec:hw-transfer}).
  \item \textbf{Small held-out sets.} Real held-out sets have 22--64 gadgets
    per class. RISC-V attacks number 27 across a handful of families, with
    wide confidence intervals.
  \item \textbf{Shortcuts.} Length, opcode mix, fence presence and fence
    position have each explained an apparent result. Every reported number
    sits next to the relevant non-learned bar.
  \item \textbf{Variance.} Per-class recall varies by up to 15--20\,pp across
    seeds for small classes. We report only multi-seed results.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\textsc{SpecExec} has a working oracle-verified generation loop on x86 that
finds many distinct leaking gadgets. It also has a detector whose headline
accuracy we have taken apart: screening, opcode shortcuts, length, the
instruction-set confound and synthetic-to-real transfer each changed what the
numbers mean. The most solid new result is a property of the data, not of a
model: on real hardware, a single lfence at the speculation boundary removes
L1TF, MDS, Spectre~V1 and Spectre~V4 leaks, and fences elsewhere do not. Three
things remain open: the learned filter has to beat simple bars on real oracle
labels, minimality has to be measured, and verification has to reach a second
ISA. Those three steps would turn the pipeline from recognition into
discovery.

\paragraph{v54 vs.\ v55.}
Locked-test accuracy was 95.75\% (v54) and 95.45\% (v55, best of 3; range
93.41--95.45\%). Fixing the augmentation raised Spectre~V2 recall from 76.0\%
to 87.7\% and Retbleed from 90.7\% to 97.3\%. L1TF recall fell from 81.1\% to
62.2\% (37 test records; 62--76\% across seeds).

\paragraph{Augmentation bugs fixed by the test suite.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Register-pool collision in rename (two registers could map to one).
  \item x86 conditional branches unguarded in local swap.
  \item Flag-clobber regex missed size suffixes.
  \item Barrier synonyms allowed downgrades (\texttt{dsb sy} $\to$
    \texttt{dsb ish}).
  \item x86 load regex missed suffixed moves.
  \item Branch-flip guard missed \texttt{jmpq *}/\texttt{callq *}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Learned edge scales.}
SPEC\_RETURN is the most amplified edge type ($s\approx1.23$--1.25) and
CONTROL\_FLOW the most suppressed ($s\approx0.74$). An edge ablation over 5
seeds shows that removing the speculative edge types costs about 0\,pp of
accuracy but $-3.75$\,pp of macro-F1. The taxonomy matters for minority
classes.

\section{Inline Features}
\label{app:inline}
The 58 inline features fall into seven groups:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item opcode fractions for 28 security-relevant mnemonics;
  \item six structural ratios;
  \item seven normalised pattern counts;
  \item ten primitive-presence flags;
  \item four ratios (e.g.\ \texttt{ret\_call\_ratio});
  \item one caller-context flag;
  \item two page-probe features (a \texttt{shl \$9--15} followed by an indexed
    load; \texttt{\$6} is a cache-line probe shared by MDS and L1TF).
\end{itemize}
\texttt{endbr64} was excluded after it increased Retbleed confusion.
